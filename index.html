What Is an IP Address? A Simple Explanation

A beginner-friendly guide to the address every internet connection lives at.

An IP address is a number that identifies a connection on a network. "IP" stands for Internet Protocol, the set of rules computers use to send information to each other. Whenever you load a web page, watch a video or send a message, the data travels in small chunks called packets, and every packet carries the address it came from and the address it is going to. Without addresses, the replies would have nowhere to go.

The postal analogy

The easiest way to picture an IP address is as a postal address for your internet connection. When you order something from a shop, you give the shop your home address so the parcel can find its way back to you. Browsing the web works the same way: your request travels out with your IP address attached, the website reads it, and sends the page back to that address. The website does not know your name or your street — it just knows the number it has to reply to.

An IPv4 address looks like four numbers separated by dots, for example 203.0.113.47. Each of the four numbers runs from 0 to 255. Newer IPv6 addresses are longer and mix numbers with letters, and the IPv4 vs IPv6 comparison explains why both formats exist.

Public and private addresses

Your home actually uses two kinds of IP address at once. The public IP address is the one the outside world sees. It belongs to your router and is assigned by your internet provider, and it is the address shown by the tool on our homepage. Every device in your house — phones, laptops, the TV — shares that single public address when it talks to the internet.

Behind the router, each device gets its own private IP address, usually something like 192.168.1.23. These addresses only work inside your home network. They let the router keep track of which device asked for which web page, a translation job called NAT (Network Address Translation). Private addresses are never visible to websites, and the same private address can be reused in millions of homes without any conflict, because they never meet on the open internet.

Who gives you your IP address?

Your internet service provider (ISP) owns large blocks of addresses and lends one to your connection. The handover is automatic: when your router connects, a system called DHCP assigns it an available address along with a "lease" — a period of time for which the address is yours. When the lease runs out it is usually renewed with the same address, but nothing guarantees that, which leads to the next point.

Dynamic vs static addresses

Most home connections have a dynamic IP address: one that can change. Restart your router, leave it off for a while, or simply wait long enough, and your provider may hand you a different address from its pool. This is completely normal and nothing to worry about — nothing on the internet depends on your address staying the same.

A static IP address never changes. Businesses pay extra for one when they run servers, security cameras or remote-access systems that other people need to reach at a fixed location. If you are not running anything like that, a dynamic address is all you need, and it is what almost every home already has.

What can someone learn from your IP address?

Less than most people fear. An IP address can usually be matched to a country, a region and often a nearby city, because providers register their address blocks publicly. It also reveals the name of your provider. That process — estimating a location from an address — is covered in detail in our guide to how websites know your location from your IP.

What an IP address does not reveal is your name, your exact home address, your phone number or anything stored on your devices. Only your provider can connect an address to a customer account, and providers only do that for formal legal requests. Even so, an IP address is a piece of your online footprint, and plenty of people prefer to mask it — one common way is to hide your IP with a VPN, and another is simply to change your IP address so the old one goes back into your provider's pool.

The short version

An IP address is the return address your connection stamps on everything it sends across the internet. Your router holds one public address that the whole household shares, each device keeps a private address indoors, and your provider can swap your public address at any time. If you have never seen yours, check your IP address now — it takes a second, and the guides on this site will make more sense with the real thing in front of you.

IPv4 vs IPv6: What Is the Difference?

Two versions of the same idea — and why the internet needs both.

If you have checked your IP address and noticed it described as IPv4 or IPv6, you have met the two generations of internet addressing. They do the same job — giving a connection an address so data can find its way back — but they were designed decades apart, for very different sizes of internet. If the basic idea of an address is new to you, start with what is an IP address and come back here afterwards.

IPv4: the original

IPv4 dates from the early 1980s, when the internet was a research network and nobody imagined it would connect billions of phones. An IPv4 address is 32 bits long and written as four decimal numbers separated by dots, like 198.51.100.23. That format allows about 4.3 billion unique addresses, which sounded like more than the world would ever need.

It was not. By the 2010s the free pool of IPv4 addresses had effectively run out. The internet kept growing anyway, largely thanks to NAT — the trick, explained in our IP address basics guide, that lets a whole household or office share one public IPv4 address. NAT bought time, but it is a workaround, not a cure.

IPv6: the replacement with room to spare

IPv6 addresses are 128 bits long and written in hexadecimal — numbers plus the letters a to f — in groups separated by colons, like 2001:db8:85a3::8a2e:370:7334. The double colon is a shortcut that replaces a run of zero groups, which is why IPv6 addresses often look shorter than they really are.

The number of possible IPv6 addresses is so large it is hard to make meaningful: roughly 340 undecillion, or enough to give every atom on Earth its own allocation and still have addresses left over. Scarcity, by design, will never be a problem again. IPv6 also builds in useful extras, such as automatic address configuration (devices can set themselves up without a DHCP server) and more efficient routing for providers.

The practical differences

  • Format: IPv4 is four short decimal numbers; IPv6 is longer, hexadecimal, and uses colons.
  • Supply: IPv4 is exhausted and recycled; IPv6 is effectively unlimited.
  • NAT: IPv4 home networks almost always hide behind NAT. IPv6 was designed so every device can have its own public address, making NAT unnecessary — though routers still firewall traffic for safety.
  • Speed: In everyday use there is no difference worth measuring. Some large networks see slightly better performance on IPv6 because routing is simpler, but your choice of provider matters far more.
  • Privacy: Early IPv6 setups built part of the address from the device's hardware ID, which could track a device across networks. Modern systems use temporary, rotating IPv6 addresses instead, so this is largely a solved problem.

Why the changeover is taking so long

The two versions cannot talk to each other directly — an IPv4-only device and an IPv6-only server are strangers without translation in between. Because of that, the internet is migrating gradually rather than switching on a single day. Most modern connections are "dual stack": your provider gives you an IPv4 address and an IPv6 address, and your devices quietly use whichever one each website supports. That is why the tool on this site shows your IPv6 address too, when your connection has one.

Providers, phone networks and big websites have been turning on IPv6 for years, and on mobile networks IPv6 traffic already exceeds IPv4 in many countries. The old version will not disappear soon — too much equipment depends on it — but every year a little more of the internet speaks the new language first.

Does it matter to you?

For everyday browsing, no. You do not need to choose, configure or even think about the version; your router and provider handle it. It is worth knowing which you have for two practical reasons. First, troubleshooting: some connection problems affect only one version, and knowing yours narrows things down. Second, privacy: masking your address with a tool that only covers IPv4 can leak your real IPv6 address alongside it — a trap our guide to what a VPN hides explains. And if you want a different address altogether, the methods in how to change your IP address apply to both versions, though not always in the same way.

How to Change Your IP Address

Five honest methods, what each one actually changes, and how to confirm it worked.

There are good ordinary reasons to want a new IP address: a website has mistakenly blocked yours, a game server has flagged it, you are troubleshooting a connection problem, or you simply do not want an old address linked to your activity any longer. The good news is that for most home connections, changing your public IP address is easy. First, note the address you have now on our homepage tool, so you can check afterwards whether it really changed.

Before you start: public vs private

"Change my IP address" almost always means the public address — the one websites see. Your devices also have private addresses inside your home network, and changing those does nothing for your visibility online. The difference is explained in what is an IP address. Every method below targets the public address.

Method 1: Restart your router (and wait)

Most home connections use dynamic addresses, handed out on a lease. When your router disconnects and reconnects, your provider may assign a different address from its pool. Turning the router off and on quickly often keeps the same address, because the lease is still valid. For a better chance, switch the router off and leave it off for a while — ten minutes helps, overnight is better — so the lease expires and the old address returns to the pool. This method is free and simple, but it is never guaranteed: some providers pin addresses for months.

Method 2: Release and renew on your computer

If your computer connects directly to a modem without a router, you can ask for a new address yourself. On Windows, open Command Prompt and run ipconfig /release followed by ipconfig /renew. On a Mac, open the network settings for your connection and click "Renew DHCP Lease". If a router sits between you and the modem — true for most homes — this only changes your private address, which websites never see, so use Method 1 instead.

Method 3: Use mobile data, or toggle airplane mode

Your phone gets its address from your mobile network, not your home provider, so switching from Wi-Fi to mobile data instantly gives you a different public IP. Toggling airplane mode on and off forces the phone to reconnect, and mobile networks frequently hand out a fresh address when that happens. This is the fastest trick when a site has blocked your phone's current address.

Method 4: Ask your provider

If restarts do not help, your provider's support line can sometimes change your address or tell you how long your lease runs. If you need an address that never changes — the opposite problem — that is a static IP, usually a paid add-on. Support can also confirm whether your connection uses IPv6, since the IPv4 and IPv6 addresses on a dual-stack connection may change independently.

Method 5: Use a VPN or a proxy

A VPN does not change the address your provider gave you — it hides it. Your traffic travels through the VPN provider's server, so websites see that server's address instead. The moment you disconnect, your real address is visible again. For many people that is exactly what they wanted, and it has the bonus of working instantly and letting you appear in another country. Our guide to what a VPN is and whether it hides your IP explains the trade-offs honestly, including what a VPN cannot do. A web proxy does a similar job for a single app or browser, usually with weaker privacy and slower speeds.

How to confirm the change worked

Come back to the IP checker on our homepage and compare. If the address matches the one you noted before you started, the method did not work — the most common reason is a router restart that was too quick to expire the lease. Also check the location shown next to it: after a genuine change, the estimated city may shift slightly, because location is guessed from the address itself, as described in how websites know your location from your IP.

A quick word of caution

Changing your IP address is a normal thing to do, but it is not a cloak of invisibility. Websites recognise returning visitors through accounts and cookies as much as through addresses, and your provider always knows which address it assigned to you. Treat a new IP as a fresh start for troubleshooting and everyday privacy hygiene — not as anonymity.

What Is a VPN and Does It Hide Your IP?

The honest version: what a VPN does, what it cannot do, and whether you need one.

VPN stands for Virtual Private Network. In plain terms, a VPN is an app that reroutes your internet traffic through a server run by the VPN company, inside an encrypted tunnel, before it reaches the websites you visit. That one detour changes two things at once: who can read your traffic along the way, and which IP address the websites at the other end get to see.

How it works, step by step

Without a VPN, your request goes from your router to your internet provider and on to the website, which replies to your public IP address. With a VPN connected, your device first builds an encrypted tunnel to the VPN server. Your requests travel through that tunnel, pop out at the server, and continue to the website from there. The website sees the VPN server's IP address and location — which could be in another country — and replies to it. Your provider, watching from the middle, can see that you are connected to a VPN, but the contents and destinations of your browsing are sealed inside the tunnel.

You can watch this happen: check your IP address with the VPN off, switch the VPN on, and refresh. The address, and usually the city and country, change. Switch the VPN off and your own address returns — the VPN borrowed you an address; it did not change yours, which is a different job described in how to change your IP address.

So, does a VPN hide your IP?

From websites, yes — that is the core of the product. A site you visit sees the VPN server's address, not yours, so it cannot use your address to estimate your real location or to recognise your household across visits. But "hide" has limits worth understanding:

  • Your provider still knows who you are. It assigned your real address and can see the tunnel exists; it just cannot see through it.
  • The VPN company can see what your provider used to see. You are moving trust, not deleting it. This is why the provider's logging policy matters.
  • Accounts and cookies still identify you. If you log into a site, it knows exactly who you are regardless of your IP address.
  • Browser fingerprinting still works. Screen size, fonts, language and dozens of other signals can single out a browser even behind a VPN.
  • Leaks can undo it. A badly configured VPN may let some traffic travel outside the tunnel, or handle only IPv4 while your IPv6 address goes out uncovered. A "kill switch" feature and IPv6 support are worth checking before you pay.

What a VPN is genuinely good for

On public Wi-Fi — cafés, airports, hotels — a VPN protects your traffic from others on the same network and from a network operator you have no reason to trust. It stops your own provider from building a profile of the sites you visit. It lets you appear in another country, which can be handy when a service you pay for behaves differently while you travel. And it gives you a clean, unblocked IP address when yours has been caught in someone else's ban — a common side effect of how IP location and blocking databases work.

What it will not do

A VPN will not make you anonymous, will not remove malware, will not stop phishing, and will not hide your activity from a service you are logged into. Marketing that promises total invisibility is selling a feeling, not a feature. Think of a VPN as a privacy tool with a specific, real job: hiding your IP address and traffic contents from the networks between you and your destination.

Choosing one, and the free VPN question

Running servers around the world costs money, so ask how a free VPN pays for itself. The honest ones are limited trials of a paid product; the dishonest ones have historically paid the bills with advertising injected into pages, or worse, by selling browsing data — the exact thing you installed it to protect. If you use a VPN at all, a reputable paid service with a clear no-logs policy, a kill switch and full IPv6 support is the safer default. Pay monthly at first, test it with the IP checker to confirm your address and location really change, and cancel if they do not.

How Websites Know Your Location from Your IP

Why sites greet you with the right country, the wrong city, and never your street.

You have probably noticed it: a shop website that opens in your currency, a streaming service that knows which country you are in, a weather page that loads a forecast for a city near you — all before you typed anything or allowed location access. In most cases, the site worked it out from your IP address. The technique is called IP geolocation, and it is cleverer and cruder than most people expect, at the same time.

There is no GPS inside an IP address

An IP address is just a number; it carries no coordinates. Geolocation works because of bookkeeping. Internet addresses are handed out in large blocks to providers and organisations, and those allocations are recorded in public registries. On top of that foundation, specialist companies build databases that map every address range to the place it is believed to be used — a country, a region, a city, and an approximate latitude and longitude.

Those companies refine their maps with extra clues: where providers say their infrastructure sits, measurements from test servers that time how long packets take to reach an address, and data partnerships with apps and websites that already know where their users are. When you look up your own IP address on this site, the city and coordinates shown come from exactly this kind of database.

How accurate is it, really?

At country level, very accurate — well above 95 percent, because address blocks are registered to providers operating in a specific country. At city level, it is a reasonable guess: often right, sometimes off by a suburb, occasionally off by hundreds of kilometres. The coordinates in a database are usually the centre of the area the address range serves, or the location of your provider's nearest hub — not your house, and not meant to be.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it worries people: an IP address cannot reveal your street address. The databases do not have it, and the technology has no way to work it out. Only your provider can connect an address to a customer's home, and that record is not public.

Why the city is sometimes wrong

  • Provider hubs: many providers route whole regions through one big city. The database sees the hub, so everyone in the region appears to live there.
  • Recycled addresses: addresses are reused as customers come and go, and a range that moved to a new area may still be listed in the old one until the databases catch up. This is also why changing your IP address can shift your apparent location.
  • Mobile networks: phone traffic often exits to the internet through a small number of national gateways, so a mobile connection can appear to be in a city far from the phone itself.
  • VPNs and proxies: if your traffic leaves through a server elsewhere, the database correctly reports that server's location — which is the point, as our VPN guide explains.

IP location is not the only signal

Websites combine several hints. If you ever clicked "allow" on a browser location prompt, the site may get a far more precise position from your device's GPS, Wi-Fi networks or mobile signal — that is a separate system with its own permission controls, and it is genuinely accurate. Language settings, time zone, currency history and account details all nudge the guess as well. IP geolocation is simply the one signal a site always has, from the very first packet, before you click or allow anything.

Why websites bother

Most uses are mundane: showing the right language, currency and prices, applying local taxes, offering the nearest store's stock, and honouring licensing rules that make some content available in one country and not another. Advertisers use it to avoid selling winter tyres in a heatwave, and security systems use it as one risk signal — a login from your usual country looks normal; one from the far side of the world, five minutes later, does not.

Can you change what sites see?

Yes. Because the location comes from the address, anything that changes the address changes the location. A VPN makes you appear wherever its server is. A new address from your provider may land in a slightly different area. And if a site has your location badly wrong in a way that matters — wrong prices, wrong catalogue — checking your IP and its estimated location on our homepage is the quickest way to confirm the cause before you blame the site.

Public vs Private IP Addresses: What Is the Difference?

Your connection has two addresses at once — here is what each one is for.

When people ask "what is my IP address?", they are usually thinking of one number. In reality, a typical home setup uses two different kinds of address at the same time: a public IP address that the internet sees, and private IP addresses that only exist inside your home. Confusing the two is the source of a lot of head-scratching — for example, when a website shows you one address and your computer's settings show a completely different one. Both are correct; they are just doing different jobs. If the basic idea of an address is still fuzzy, our guide to what an IP address is is a good place to start.

The public IP address: your connection's face to the world

Your public IP address is assigned by your internet service provider and belongs to your connection — normally to your router. It is globally unique: no two connections on the internet use the same public address at the same time. Every website you visit sees this address, because it is the return address stamped on all the traffic leaving your home. It is the address shown by the checker on our homepage, and it is the one involved when people talk about location estimates, VPNs or changing their IP.

Because the whole household shares this single address, every device in your home — your phone, your partner's laptop, the smart TV, a visitor's tablet — appears to websites as the same public IP. That is normal, and it is one reason an IP address can never identify a specific person or device on its own, a point we return to in our article on what someone can actually do with your IP address.

The private IP address: indoor numbers

Inside your home network, your router hands each device its own private IP address. These come from special ranges set aside for private use and never routed on the open internet:

  • 192.168.0.0 to 192.168.255.255 — the range almost every home router uses, giving addresses like 192.168.1.42.
  • 10.0.0.0 to 10.255.255.255 — common in larger networks and some providers' routers.
  • 172.16.0.0 to 172.31.255.255 — less common at home, often seen in offices and virtual machines.

Because these ranges are private, they can be reused in every home on the planet without clashing. Your neighbour's laptop can be 192.168.1.42 too, and neither of you will ever know, because those addresses never meet outside your respective front doors. Private addresses let the router keep track of which device asked for which web page, so replies go to the right screen. The translation between the private addresses indoors and the one public address outdoors is done by NAT, which has its own guide: what NAT is and how it works.

How to tell them apart at a glance

If an address falls in one of the three ranges above, it is private. Anything else is public. There are also a few special-purpose addresses worth recognising: 127.0.0.1 is "localhost", a device talking to itself, and addresses starting with 169.254 are self-assigned fallbacks a device gives itself when it cannot get a proper address — a useful clue when troubleshooting, as our guide to IP address conflicts explains. On the newer system, IPv6 has its own private-style ranges (addresses starting with fe80 are local-only), though IPv6 was designed so devices can also hold a genuine public address each.

Which one do websites, apps and games see?

Websites only ever see your public address. Your private address stays inside your home, and no setting or trick can expose it to a website directly (one browser technology called WebRTC historically could reveal it, which is why it features in our guide to checking whether your VPN is leaking). Private addresses matter for things that happen indoors: connecting to your printer, opening your router's settings page, sharing files between your own computers, or joining a game hosted on a friend's machine on the same network. Step-by-step instructions for finding both kinds of address on each of your devices are in our guide to finding your IP on any device.

Why the distinction matters

Knowing which address is which clears up most IP confusion. If a service has blocked "your IP", it means the public one — and since that is shared, a block can be caused by someone else in the household, or even a previous customer of your provider who had the address before you. If a smart device cannot be reached, the problem is almost certainly with private addressing indoors. And if you want a new address that websites will notice, only the public one counts, which is why the methods in how to change your IP address all target it. Two addresses, two different worlds — once you keep them separate in your mind, the rest of how home networking works falls into place.

Static vs Dynamic IP Addresses Explained

Why yours keeps changing, why a server's never does, and whether you should care.

Look up your IP address today, then look again in a month, and there is a good chance the number will be different. That is not a fault — it is how most home connections are designed to work. IP addresses come in two flavours: dynamic, meaning they can change, and static, meaning they stay fixed. Almost every home connection uses a dynamic address, while the servers that run websites use static ones. Here is why the system works that way, and the few situations where a static address is worth paying for.

Dynamic addresses and the lease system

A dynamic address is lent to your connection rather than given to it. When your router connects, your provider's systems assign it an available address from a shared pool, along with a lease — a set period, anything from a few hours to a few weeks, for which that address is yours. The machinery behind this is called DHCP, and it is covered in our guide to how IP addresses are assigned.

While the lease lasts and your router stays connected, the address usually renews quietly and nothing changes. But if the router disconnects past the end of a lease — or the provider reshuffles its pool — the old address goes back and a fresh one is handed out. That is the whole story behind the common experience described in why your IP changes when you restart your router: nothing broke, the lease simply ended.

Providers like this system for practical reasons. Addresses are a finite resource — especially IPv4 addresses, which ran out years ago — and not every customer is online at once. A pool that recycles addresses serves more customers than giving everyone a permanent one. It also means your provider can reorganise its network without writing to every household.

Static addresses: fixed on purpose

A static IP address is reserved for one customer and never changes, through restarts, outages and lease renewals. It is configured once and stays put until someone deliberately changes it. Businesses buy static addresses when other people or systems need to reach them reliably at a known location: a company web or email server, a remote-access gateway for staff, security cameras checked from a phone, or another business's firewall that only allows connections from approved addresses.

Static addresses are almost always a paid extra on top of a business-grade connection, and some providers will not sell one on a residential plan at all. They also carry a small trade-off: because the address never changes, it is a stable identifier for your connection over time, which is the opposite of what privacy-conscious users want — a point explored in IP addresses and online privacy.

A middle option: the "sticky" dynamic address

In practice, many home connections sit between the two extremes. The address is technically dynamic, but the provider's systems keep handing the same one back to the same router for months at a time, because the lease renews without interruption. Users often assume they have a static address until a long power cut or a provider upgrade finally changes it. If you are relying on your address staying the same — for a home server, say — do not trust this luck: either pay for a static address or use a dynamic DNS service, which updates a hostname every time your address changes. DNS itself is explained in what DNS is and how it relates to your IP.

Do you need a static IP?

For ordinary home use — browsing, streaming, gaming, video calls, working from home — the honest answer is no. Nothing you do day to day depends on your address staying fixed, and services you log into recognise you by your account, not your address. A dynamic address is even mildly helpful: it makes your connection a slightly moving target and gives you an easy reset when an address picks up a bad reputation, since you can usually change your IP address with a router restart.

You might genuinely need a static address if you host something others connect to from outside — a game server for friends, a self-hosted file service, cameras you check remotely — or if a work system only accepts connections from whitelisted addresses. Even then, check whether the thing you are hosting works through your router's settings first; many modern services use outbound connections and cloud relays precisely so home users do not need static addresses at all.

The short version

Dynamic means borrowed and changeable; static means reserved and fixed. Homes get dynamic addresses because it is efficient and they lose nothing by it. Servers and businesses pay for static addresses because being findable at the same number forever is the whole point. If you are not sure which you have, note your address on our homepage, restart your router, and check again — though as the lease system shows, even that test can take a few tries to prove anything.

How to Find Your IP Address on Windows, Mac, Android and iPhone

Step-by-step instructions for your public address and your local one, on every device.

Every device you own has IP addresses, and sooner or later something will ask you for one: a troubleshooting guide, a printer setup, a game server, a support call. There are two addresses you might need. Your public IP address is the one the internet sees, and it is the same for every device on your home connection. Your private (local) IP address is the one your router gave that specific device for use inside your home. The difference is explained in public vs private IP addresses. Finding the public one is identical on every device; finding the local one differs, so this guide goes device by device.

Find your public IP address (any device)

This takes five seconds and needs nothing installed. Open your browser on the device and visit our homepage. The tool there asks public detection services what address your connection is using and shows it in large type, along with its version, approximate location and provider. Because the public address belongs to your connection rather than the device, you will get the same answer on your phone, your laptop and your TV while they are all on your home Wi-Fi. Switch the phone to mobile data and check again, and you will see a different address — that one belongs to your mobile network.

Windows: find your local IP address

The quickest route uses Settings:

  1. Open Settings and go to Network & internet.
  2. Click Wi-Fi (or Ethernet if you use a cable), then click the name of the network you are connected to.
  3. Scroll down to the properties list. Your local address is the value labelled IPv4 address — it will usually look like 192.168.1.x. An IPv6 address, if your network uses one, is listed just above it.

Alternatively, open Command Prompt, type ipconfig and press Enter. Under your active connection, the IPv4 Address line shows the same thing, and the Default Gateway line shows your router's own local address — handy, because typing that into a browser opens the router's settings page.

Mac: find your local IP address

  1. Open System Settings (called System Preferences on older macOS versions).
  2. Click Wi-Fi in the sidebar, then click Details next to the network you are connected to. For a wired connection, click Network and select the Ethernet adapter instead.
  3. Your local address is shown as the IP address value, typically 192.168.1.x, with the router's address listed beside it.

If you are comfortable with the Terminal app, the command ipconfig getifaddr en0 prints your Wi-Fi address on a single line — a favourite trick of support technicians.

Android: find your local IP address

Menu names vary slightly between manufacturers, but the path is similar on most phones:

  1. Open Settings and tap Network & internet (or Connections on Samsung phones), then Wi-Fi.
  2. Tap the name of the network you are connected to, or the gear icon beside it.
  3. Expand Advanced if needed. Your address appears as IP address, usually 192.168.1.x.

You can also go to Settings → About phone → Status, which lists the IP address on many Android versions. Note that modern Android phones randomise their MAC address per network for privacy, but the local IP address works exactly as it always has.

iPhone and iPad: find your local IP address

  1. Open Settings and tap Wi-Fi.
  2. Tap the blue (i) icon next to the network you are connected to.
  3. Under the IPv4 Address heading, your address is the value labelled IP Address — typically 192.168.1.x. The router's address is on the line below it.

If the network supports IPv6, you will also see one or more IPv6 addresses listed further down the same screen.

Which address did you actually need?

If a website, a colleague or a support agent asks for "your IP", they almost always mean the public one — get it from the homepage tool. If you are setting up something inside your home — a printer, a NAS drive, a locally hosted game — you need the local one from the steps above. And if a device shows no address at all, or one starting with 169.254, it failed to get an address from the router; restarting the device, then the router, fixes it in most cases, and our guide to IP address conflicts covers the related error messages you might see.

What Can Someone Do With Your IP Address? An Honest Answer

Less than the scary posts claim, more than nothing — the calm, accurate version.

Few topics collect as much nonsense as this one. Search around and you will find claims that anyone who learns your IP address can empty your bank account, watch you through your camera, or turn up at your door. You will also find the opposite claim, that an IP address is completely meaningless and worthless to anyone. Neither is true. This article gives the honest version: what an IP address genuinely reveals, what someone could realistically do with it, and what is flatly impossible — so you can judge for yourself how much it deserves your worry. To see the address in question, check your own IP first.

What an IP address does reveal

An IP address can be looked up in public databases — the same kind this site uses — and a lookup typically returns:

  • Your country, with very high reliability.
  • Your approximate region and city — often right, sometimes off by a suburb, occasionally off by a long way. The mechanics, and the limits, are covered in how websites know your location from your IP.
  • Your internet provider's name, and whether the connection looks like a home line, a business line, a mobile network or a data centre.
  • That the address is online, and roughly when — an address responds to traffic only while the connection using it is active.

That is the full list. Note what it sounds like: it describes a connection, in an area, run by a company. It does not describe a person.

What someone could realistically do with it

Approximately locate you. As above: country reliably, city roughly. Enough for someone to say "this person is probably in the Manchester area", never enough to find a street.

Send unwanted traffic at your connection. The most genuinely annoying misuse is a denial-of-service attack: flooding your connection with junk traffic so your real traffic cannot get through. This is a known nuisance in competitive gaming, where an opponent who obtains a player's IP can knock them offline mid-match. It is disruptive but temporary — it damages nothing, steals nothing, and it stops the moment the flood stops or your address changes. Since most home addresses are dynamic, a router restart usually ends it for good; see how to change your IP address.

Probe your connection for open doors. Someone can scan your public address to see whether any services are exposed to the internet. On a normal home setup the answer is almost always "none", because your router's firewall and NAT mean the internet cannot reach your devices directly at all. This becomes a real concern mainly for people who have deliberately opened ports — for a home server, say — without securing what sits behind them.

Recognise your household across websites. While your address stays the same, sites and advertisers can treat it as a rough household identifier. Because the whole home shares one public address, though, it points at a household at most — never at which person in it did what. The wider picture is covered in IP addresses and online privacy.

What an IP address cannot do

Now the part the scary posts leave out. With only your IP address, nobody can:

  • Find your name, home address, phone number or email address. That mapping exists only in your provider's customer records, which are not public.
  • Hack into your computer, phone or accounts. An address is a destination, not a key. Getting into a device requires an exposed, vulnerable service or tricking you into installing something — a completely separate problem that an IP alone does not solve.
  • See what you browse, read your messages, or access your files or photos.
  • Watch you through your camera or listen through your microphone.
  • Spend your money or impersonate you to your bank.

Every one of those horrors requires something else: malware on your device, a stolen password, a phished login, an unsecured service you exposed. If someone online claims they "have your IP and will hack you", what they actually have is a number that says which provider you use and roughly which city you are in.

The one exception worth knowing

Your provider can connect an IP address, plus a date and time, to a customer account — because it assigned the address. That is why law enforcement, with a valid legal order, can ask a provider who used a given address at a given time. It is also why the realistic privacy question about IP addresses is not "what can a random stranger do" but "who keeps records, and for how long" — which is a question about your provider and the sites you visit, addressed in how websites use your IP address. For everyone else, the trail stops at the provider's door.

Sensible precautions, in proportion

There is no need to panic about your IP address, and no need to pay anyone to "protect" it. A few cheap habits cover the real risks: do not post your IP publicly in screenshots or streams; keep your router's firmware updated and do not open ports you do not understand; if you are being flooded during games, restart the router for a fresh address or use a VPN, which puts the VPN server's address in the line of fire instead of yours. That is genuinely all there is to it. Your IP address is closer to your postcode district than to your house keys — mildly informative, occasionally inconvenient, and nowhere near as powerful as the internet's storytellers would have you believe.

What Is DNS and How Does It Relate to Your IP Address?

The internet's phone book — and why it matters for both speed and privacy.

Computers reach each other using IP addresses, but humans are hopeless at remembering strings of numbers. You type a name like example.com; your device needs the address behind it before anything can load. The system that translates names into addresses is DNS — the Domain Name System — and it quietly handles one of these translations for almost everything you do online. Understanding it explains a handful of everyday mysteries: why a site can move servers without you noticing, why changing two numbers in your settings can unblock or speed up your browsing, and why privacy guides keep mentioning DNS alongside your IP address.

DNS in one paragraph

DNS works like a phone book, or a contacts list. When you type a website's name, your device asks a DNS resolver — a server usually run by your internet provider — "what is the IP address for this name?" The resolver either answers from its memory of recent lookups or goes and asks a chain of other servers: the root of the DNS system, the registry for the ending (such as .com), and finally the website's own nameserver, which gives the definitive answer. The whole conversation normally takes a few hundredths of a second, the answer is remembered (cached) for a while, and your device then connects directly to the IP address it was given. If what an IP address is is still new to you, read that guide first — DNS is simply the directory that sits in front of it.

How DNS relates to your IP address

The relationship runs in both directions. Looking outward, DNS gives you the IP addresses of the services you visit — every website, app backend and mail server you use has an address that DNS found for you. Looking inward, there is a lesser-known trick called reverse DNS, which works the other way: given an IP address, it can sometimes return a name associated with it. Look up your own IP address and its reverse name, when one exists, usually looks like a scrambled version of your provider's domain — something containing your ISP's name and sometimes a city code. It reveals nothing personal, but it is one more way an address is tied to a provider rather than a person.

There is also a practical connection for people whose home address changes. Because most home connections use dynamic IP addresses, a service called dynamic DNS exists: a small program at home watches your public address and updates a personal hostname — like yourname.ddns.example — every time the address changes, so you can always reach your home network by name. It is the standard workaround for not having a static address.

Your DNS queries are a browsing history in miniature

Here is the part privacy guides care about. Before your device connects to a site, it asks the resolver for that site's address — by name, traditionally in plain text. Whoever runs your resolver can therefore see the names of essentially every service you use, and for years that meant your provider, whether or not you used a VPN for the actual browsing. The website names alone do not reveal which pages you read or what you typed, but as a list of destinations they are revealing enough.

Two developments have improved this. Encrypted DNS — standards called DNS over HTTPS and DNS over TLS — wraps the questions and answers in encryption, and modern browsers and phones increasingly turn it on by default, often using large public resolvers. And a properly configured VPN carries DNS queries through its tunnel to the VPN company's resolver, so your provider sees neither the queries nor the browsing. A VPN that fails to do this is "leaking" DNS, one of the faults checked in our guide to checking if your VPN is leaking.

Why people change their DNS servers

Your resolver is normally your provider's, set automatically when your router connects — part of the same handover described in how IP addresses are assigned. But you can point your devices or router at public resolvers run by companies such as Cloudflare (1.1.1.1) or Google (8.8.8.8). Reasons people do: speed, if the provider's resolver is sluggish; reliability, if it fails oddly; filtering, since some resolvers block known malware domains; and occasionally access, because a provider's DNS-based blocking of a site disappears when you stop asking that provider. Note what changing DNS does not do: it does not change your IP address, does not hide you from websites, and does not encrypt your browsing. It changes who answers your phone-book questions — nothing more, but nothing less either.

When DNS is the problem

A surprising number of "the internet is down" moments are DNS failures: your connection is fine, but names stop resolving, so every site appears broken while apps already connected keep working. The classic fixes are restarting the router, switching temporarily to a public resolver, or flushing your device's DNS cache. And if a website has just moved to a new server, stale cached answers can send you to the old address for a while — the internet's phone book is updated constantly, but every copy of it expires at its own pace.

What Is a Proxy Server and How Is It Different From a VPN?

Both lend you a different IP address. Almost everything else about them differs.

A proxy server and a VPN are often mentioned in the same breath, because from a website's point of view they do the same headline trick: the site sees the middleman's IP address instead of yours. Underneath, they are quite different tools, built for different jobs, with different strengths and very different privacy implications. If you have read our guide to what a VPN is, this article is the natural companion: it explains what a proxy actually does, the main kinds you will meet, and how to decide which of the two — if either — you need.

What a proxy server does

A proxy is a server that passes requests along on your behalf. You configure an app — usually just your web browser — to send its traffic to the proxy first. The proxy then fetches the page using its own connection and passes the result back to you. The website at the far end sees the proxy's IP address and location, exactly as it would with a VPN, so a proxy in another country makes you appear to be in that country for the apps using it. You can verify the effect in seconds: set up a proxy, then check your IP address in the browser you configured — and notice that a different browser, or another app, still shows your real address, because the proxy only covers the traffic sent to it.

The kinds of proxy you will meet

  • HTTP/HTTPS proxies handle web traffic for the browser or app configured to use them. Simple, widely supported, and limited to that app.
  • SOCKS proxies are more general-purpose: they pass along many kinds of traffic (games, chat apps, download tools), not just web pages, but still only for apps individually configured.
  • Transparent proxies are ones you did not choose: some workplaces, schools, hotels and providers route traffic through a proxy for filtering or caching without asking your device at all.
  • Web proxies are websites themselves — you type a URL into a page and it fetches it for you. Convenient for a one-off look at a blocked page, and the least trustworthy kind on this list.
  • Residential proxies route traffic through real home connections, making the traffic look like an ordinary household's. This is a large commercial market, and its supply chain — how those home connections were recruited — ranges from legitimate to deeply questionable.

Proxy vs VPN: the real differences

Scope. A proxy covers one app at a time, and only if that app respects proxy settings. A VPN covers the whole device — every app, every background process — because it works at the network level, rerouting traffic through an encrypted tunnel before apps are involved.

Encryption. This is the big one. A basic proxy does not encrypt your traffic: between you and the proxy, anyone watching the network — your provider, the café Wi-Fi owner — can see as much as they could without it. A VPN encrypts everything between you and the VPN server by design. (An HTTPS connection to a website is encrypted either way, but the destinations you visit are still visible on an unencrypted path.)

Speed and simplicity. Proxies are usually lighter: no tunnel overhead, quick to switch on for a single task, often faster for simple fetching. VPNs add some overhead but give you system-wide consistency.

Trust. Both kinds of middleman can, in principle, see the traffic they carry for you. Free proxies are notorious for this: running servers costs money, and the historical record of free proxy services includes injected advertising and harvested data. The same caution applies to free VPNs, but the paid VPN market is more established and more scrutinised than the free proxy world.

So which do you need?

Choose a proxy when the job is narrow and the stakes are low: making one app appear in another country, getting around a simple network block for a single task, or routing a specific tool's traffic through a specific place. Choose a VPN when the job is privacy or security: protecting everything on an untrusted network, hiding your destinations from your provider, or keeping your real address consistently hidden across all apps — including ones that might otherwise leak it, as described in how to check if your VPN is leaking. And if your real goal is a different address rather than a hidden one, neither tool is strictly required: the simpler methods in how to change your IP address may do the job for free.

The shortest honest summary: a proxy is a forwarding address for one app; a VPN is an armoured tunnel for the whole device. Both change the IP address websites see. Only one of them was built to keep the journey private.

How IP Addresses Are Assigned: IANA, ISPs and DHCP

From a global registry to the router in your hallway — the full journey of an address.

Your IP address was not chosen by your router, invented by your computer, or picked at random by the internet. It travelled down a chain of custody that starts with a global coordinator and ends at your front door, and every step of that chain is deliberately organised so that no two connections on Earth use the same public address at the same time. This article follows an address down that chain, and explains the automatic system — DHCP — that performs the final handover every time you connect. You can see the end result any time on our homepage.

Step one: IANA and the regional registries

At the top sits IANA, the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority, which holds the master pool of IP addresses. IANA does not hand addresses to companies or people directly. Instead, it allocates huge blocks to five regional internet registries, each covering a part of the world: ARIN for North America, RIPE NCC for Europe, the Middle East and Central Asia, APNIC for Asia-Pacific, LACNIC for Latin America, and AFRINIC for Africa. This geography is one reason IP addresses carry location information at all — the system was divided by region from the start, which the databases described in how websites know your location from your IP take advantage of.

Step two: your provider's allocation

Internet service providers, hosting companies and large organisations get their address blocks from their regional registry. A big ISP might hold millions of addresses; a small one, a few thousand. The provider then subdivides its allocation internally — some for home broadband customers in one city, some for business lines, some for its own equipment — and registers the broad strokes publicly, which is how a lookup can tell you both the provider's name and roughly where an address is used. When IPv4 ran out, this layer is where the pain was felt: registries had no more large blocks to give, and providers began trading existing addresses, stretching them with NAT, and rolling out IPv6 alongside.

Step three: DHCP, the automatic handover

The final step happens in seconds, every time your router connects, and it is handled by DHCP — the Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol. The conversation goes roughly like this. Your router announces itself on the provider's network and asks for configuration. A DHCP server answers with an offer: an IP address from the pool set aside for your area, plus the other settings the router needs — the gateway to send traffic to, and the DNS servers to use for turning names into addresses. The router accepts, and the address is recorded as leased to your connection.

Leased is the key word. The address comes with an expiry time, and your router periodically renews the lease to keep it. As long as renewals succeed, the address stays the same, which is why a "dynamic" address can survive for months. If the router is offline when the lease expires, the address returns to the pool for someone else, and the next connection gets whatever is free — the mechanism behind why restarting your router can change your IP. The whole dynamic-versus-static picture is rounded up in static vs dynamic IP addresses explained.

The same thing happens again inside your home

Your router runs a second, miniature version of this system for your household. It is also a DHCP server: when your phone joins the Wi-Fi, it asks the router for an address, and the router lends it one from its private pool — typically 192.168.1.x — along with a lease. That is why a device's local address can change after it has been away or off for a long time, and why two devices occasionally end up claiming the same local address, the glitch covered in what an IP address conflict is and how to fix it. The chain, in full, is: IANA to registry, registry to provider, provider to your router, router to your devices — four handovers between a global pool and the phone in your hand.

What about IPv6?

IPv6 follows the same chain of custody at the top — IANA, registries, providers — but the final step is more relaxed. Because IPv6 addresses are effectively unlimited, providers hand out whole blocks (prefixes) to each household rather than a single address, and devices can even configure their own addresses automatically by combining the household prefix with a self-generated part, a process called SLAAC, with DHCPv6 as an alternative. The result is the same from your side — you connect and you have addresses — but the scarcity-driven leasing dance of IPv4 mostly disappears.

Why this organisation matters to you

Mostly, it explains things you can observe. It is why your address points to your provider and your rough location: the address literally came from the block that provider registered for your region. It is why your address can change without anything being wrong: the lease system working as designed. And it is why no website can turn your address into your name: the final link in the chain — which customer held which address, and when — exists only in your provider's own lease records, and it is the one link that is not public.

What Is NAT? Network Address Translation Explained Simply

The clever trick that lets one address serve your whole household.

Your home has one public IP address, but it may have a dozen devices online at once — phones, laptops, a TV, a games console, a doorbell. All of them send and receive their own traffic through that single address, without their data getting mixed up. The technology that makes this work is NAT, Network Address Translation, and it runs inside your router, silently, every second of the day. NAT is also the reason the internet did not collapse when IPv4 addresses ran out, and the reason your devices are surprisingly hard to reach from outside. Here is how it works, in plain English.

The receptionist analogy

Picture an office with one phone number and fifty staff. Callers dial the single public number and reach a receptionist, who transfers each call to the right person's extension. NAT is your router doing that job for data. Inside the office, each device has its own extension — its private IP address, like 192.168.1.23. Outside, the whole household has one number: the public address shown by our homepage tool.

When your laptop asks a website for a page, the router rewrites the request before sending it out: it replaces the laptop's private address with the household's public address, and makes a note in a table — "this conversation belongs to the laptop". The website replies to the public address, the router checks its table, rewrites the destination back to the laptop's private address, and delivers it. Your phone does the same thing a second later, and the table gains another row. Because the router also varies the port numbers attached to each conversation, thousands of simultaneous conversations can share one address without confusion.

Why NAT exists: the IPv4 shortage

NAT became universal because the alternative — giving every device its own public IPv4 address — stopped being possible. There are only about 4.3 billion IPv4 addresses, and far more devices than that. NAT stretches the supply enormously: one address per household instead of one per device. Some providers take this further with carrier-grade NAT, where whole streets of customers share a handful of public addresses behind the provider's own translation layer — which is why your public address sometimes appears to be shared, why its location can look odd, and why some home-hosted services refuse to work no matter what you configure on your own router.

NAT's accidental superpower: a one-way door

Because the router only delivers incoming traffic that matches an entry in its table — a conversation a device inside started — unexpected traffic from the internet has nowhere to go and is dropped. This gives every home network a basic firewall effect for free: the internet cannot simply knock on your laptop's door, because as far as the internet is concerned, your laptop has no address. This is a large part of why the probing described in what someone can do with your IP address usually achieves nothing against a home connection.

The one-way door can be opened deliberately. Port forwarding tells the router "when traffic arrives on this port, send it to this device" — needed for hosting a game server or reaching a home camera. UPnP lets devices open such doors automatically, which is convenient and, if a rogue device or program abuses it, risky; many security guides suggest turning UPnP off and forwarding ports manually only when you understand what you are exposing.

Where NAT causes trouble

Gamers know NAT best through its error messages. Consoles grade NAT as open, moderate or strict depending on how easily incoming connections can be arranged, and two players behind strict NAT may fail to connect to each other for voice chat or peer-to-peer games — both sides are behind one-way doors, waiting for the other to knock. Double NAT is another classic: two routers in a chain (a provider's box plus your own router, say) each translating, which breaks port forwarding until one is switched to bridge mode. And some older applications that embed IP addresses inside their data — certain voice, file-transfer and VPN setups — need special handling because the address inside the message no longer matches the one NAT wrote on the outside.

Does IPv6 make NAT go away?

In principle, yes: IPv6 has enough addresses for every device to have its own public one, and the translation layer becomes unnecessary — connections can be direct again, with the router acting purely as a firewall to keep unwanted traffic out. In practice, NAT is so deeply embedded in how networks are built that it will be with us for years, and some networks use it in IPv6 too. Either way, you never need to configure NAT to use the internet; it is simply the quiet machinery that makes your one address serve the whole household. If you are curious which private addresses it is juggling in your home, our guide to finding your IP on any device shows where each device lists its own.

How to Check If Your VPN Is Leaking Your Real IP Address

Three quick tests that show whether your VPN is doing its one essential job.

A VPN has one headline promise: websites should see the VPN server's IP address, not yours. Most of the time it keeps that promise. But there are several well-known ways a VPN setup can quietly fail — an uncovered IPv6 connection, DNS requests slipping outside the tunnel, a browser feature bypassing the VPN entirely — and fail silently, leaving you believing you are hidden when you are not. The good news is that every one of these leaks is easy to test for, using nothing but your browser. This guide walks through the three checks that matter. If you have not already, skim what a VPN is and what it hides first, so the results make sense.

Before you test: get your real address

Disconnect the VPN completely and visit our homepage. Write down exactly what it shows: your real IP address (and its IPv4 and IPv6 versions if both appear), your city and country, and your provider's name. This is your baseline. Every leak test below amounts to the same question: with the VPN connected, does anything still match this baseline?

Test 1: the basic IP check

Now connect to your VPN — ideally to a server in a different country, so the change is unmistakable — and refresh the homepage. You should see the VPN server's address, in the VPN server's city and country, with a data-centre provider rather than your home ISP. If the address, city or provider still matches your baseline, the VPN is not routing your traffic at all: check that it actually connected, that it is not in a paused or split-tunnel mode that excludes your browser, and that you do not have a proxy setting somewhere overriding it. This single test catches total failures, but passing it does not mean you are leak-free — the next two tests catch the partial ones.

Test 2: the IPv6 check

Many connections today are dual-stack: you have an IPv4 address and an IPv6 address at once. Some VPNs — especially older or cheaper ones — tunnel only the IPv4 traffic and leave IPv6 to travel directly, which means any website that prefers IPv6 sees your real address and location even while your IPv4 result looks perfectly hidden. Our homepage tool reports both versions when it can, so compare carefully: with the VPN on, an IPv6 address from your real provider should not appear anywhere in the result. If it does, look in the VPN's settings for an IPv6 toggle — enabling IPv6 support, or disabling IPv6 leak protection's opposite (some apps phrase it as "block IPv6") — or, as a blunt fix, disable IPv6 in your device's network settings while you rely on the VPN. This is the single most common leak on modern connections.

Test 3: the DNS check

Even when web traffic goes through the tunnel, your device's DNS requests — the lookups that turn site names into addresses — might still be sent to your provider's resolver, in the clear. That does not expose your IP address to websites, but it hands your provider a list of every service you look up, defeating much of the point. To test, use one of the free "DNS leak test" pages offered by several VPN companies and privacy sites: it will list the resolver servers your connection is using. With the VPN on, those resolvers should belong to the VPN company (or to a neutral public resolver you chose), and be located in the VPN server's country — not your provider's resolvers in your real city. If your provider's name appears, enable the VPN's DNS leak protection, or set a trusted resolver manually in your device's settings.

Test 4: the WebRTC check

WebRTC is a browser technology for voice and video calls that, by design, discovers the addresses your device can be reached at — including, historically, your real and even your private local addresses, regardless of a VPN. Modern browsers have tightened this considerably and now mask local addresses in most cases, but it is still worth checking: "WebRTC leak test" pages will show any addresses your browser reveals. With the VPN on, nothing shown should match your baseline. If it does, your options are to disable WebRTC in the browser (possible directly in Firefox, via an extension or flag in Chrome-based browsers), or use the VPN provider's own browser extension, which usually handles it. Note that disabling WebRTC may break some in-browser calling and meeting tools, so test those afterwards if you rely on them.

And one behaviour to verify: the kill switch

Finally, test what happens when the VPN drops — because connections do drop. Connect the VPN, start a download or stream, then disconnect the VPN abruptly (or toggle your Wi-Fi off and on to force a reconnect). A VPN with a working kill switch blocks traffic until the tunnel is back, rather than letting your apps continue on your naked connection. Some exposure for a few seconds is exactly the kind of quiet failure these tests exist to catch.

If your setup passes all of the above, your VPN is doing its job properly: your address, your DNS and your browser all present the VPN's identity, not yours. If it fails one and the settings cannot fix it, that is a genuine reason to switch providers — a VPN that leaks is worse than none, because it replaces caution with false confidence. Either way, the baseline test takes ten seconds, so re-run it whenever you change VPN apps, servers or devices: check your IP address, and compare.

IP Addresses and Online Privacy: The Basics Everyone Should Know

Where your IP address really ranks among the things that identify you online.

Your IP address is part of your online privacy picture — but it is not the whole picture, and it is not even the biggest part. Privacy advice tends to obsess over IP addresses because they are visible and easy to explain, while the identifiers that actually follow you around the internet — accounts, cookies, browser fingerprints — are quieter. This article puts your IP address in its proper place: what it exposes, who can see it, how long it can be linked to you, and the habits that genuinely improve your privacy, in the order they matter. Start by seeing your own IP address as the sites you visit see it.

What your IP address exposes, exactly

Every server you connect to receives your public IP address — it has to, or it would not know where to send the page back. Along with it, a lookup can add your approximate location (country reliably, city roughly) and your provider's name, as explained in how websites know your location from your IP. That is the exposure: a rough where, and which company connects you. Not your name, not your address, not the contents of anything. Our article on what someone can do with your IP address goes through the possibilities one by one, calmly.

Why it still counts as personal information

Privacy law in many places treats IP addresses as personal data, and there is a fair reason: combined with a timestamp, your provider can match an address to your customer account, because it assigned the address to you — the chain is described in how IP addresses are assigned. So while an address alone identifies a connection, an address plus provider records identifies a subscriber. That is why websites log addresses carefully, why courts argue about them, and why "it is just a number" is as incomplete as "it reveals everything". The number is a link in a chain — weak on its own, stronger when joined with records only a provider holds.

The identifiers that matter more

If privacy is your goal, keep the ranking straight:

  • Your accounts. When you are logged in, the service knows exactly who you are. No IP trick changes that.
  • Cookies and similar storage. These recognise your browser across visits and often across sites, long after your IP has changed.
  • Browser and device fingerprinting. Your screen size, fonts, language, time zone and dozens of other settings combine into a near-unique profile that works even behind a VPN.
  • Your IP address. Real, but blunt: it identifies a household's connection, changes over time, and is shared by everyone in the home.

This is why changing your IP feels satisfying but accomplishes limited privacy on its own: the other three identifiers simply carry on. It is also why a VPN should be understood as one tool among several, not a invisibility switch.

How long does an IP stay "yours"?

For most home connections, your address is dynamic: it changes when leases expire, routers restart, or providers reshuffle — sometimes weekly, sometimes after months. That churn limits how useful an old address is for tracking you: an address you had in March may belong to a stranger across town by June. It also cuts the other way for accountability: an accusation based on an address from months ago is meaningless without the provider's records of who held it then. On mobile networks the churn is faster still, with addresses often shared among many subscribers at once through carrier-grade NAT.

Habits that genuinely help

Use a VPN on networks you do not control. On café, hotel and airport Wi-Fi, a VPN hides your traffic and destinations from the network operator and other users — the clearest win a VPN offers. At home it mainly shifts trust from your provider to the VPN company, so choose one deliberately, and verify it with the tests in how to check if your VPN is leaking.

Mind the bigger identifiers. Log out of accounts you are not using, clear or block third-party cookies, and consider a browser with strong anti-fingerprinting defaults if tracking concerns you. These steps address the top of the ranking above, where most real-world identification happens.

Use encrypted connections and DNS. HTTPS (the padlock) keeps page contents private from the networks in between; encrypted DNS stops your lookups being readable or logged by whoever runs the resolver — see what DNS is and how it relates to your IP.

Keep perspective. Do not post your IP publicly, do not pay for scareware "IP protection", and do not let address anxiety distract from passwords, updates and phishing — the things that actually compromise people. Your IP address deserves a sensible paragraph in your privacy thinking, not the whole chapter.

What Is a Subnet and a Subnet Mask? A Plain-English Guide

The idea behind 255.255.255.0 and /24, explained without the maths headache.

Sooner or later, anyone who pokes around in network settings meets two mysterious items: a "subnet mask" like 255.255.255.0, and addresses written like 192.168.1.0/24. They look like the kind of thing only network engineers should touch — but the idea underneath is simple, and once you have it, a lot of home networking behaviour (including why your devices can talk to each other but not to your neighbour's) suddenly makes sense. This guide explains subnets the plain way, using your own home network as the example.

An IP address has two parts

Every IP address quietly contains two pieces of information: which network the device is on, and which device it is within that network. Think of a phone number with an area code: the area code says which region you are in, and the rest says which line. Or think of a street address: the street name is the network part, the house number is the device part. The problem is that the boundary between the two parts is not fixed — it can sit in different places depending on how the network was set up. Telling devices where the boundary sits is the subnet mask's entire job. If what an IP address is needs a refresher first, start there.

The subnet mask, translated

A subnet mask is a companion number that sits next to every IP address. Where the mask has its "on" portion, the address is the network part; where the mask is "off", the address is the device part. The home-network classic, 255.255.255.0, applied to an address like 192.168.1.42, says: the first three chunks — 192.168.1 — are the network, and the last chunk — 42 — is this particular device. So every device whose address starts with 192.168.1. is on the same local network (the same "street"), and the final number, from 1 to 254, is its house number on that street.

The shorthand /24 says the same thing more compactly: it counts how many of the address's 32 bits belong to the network part. 255.255.255.0 is 24 bits of "on", hence 192.168.1.0/24. A /24 network has room for 254 usable device addresses (256 minus the network address itself, .0, and the broadcast address, .255, which talks to everyone at once) — far more than any household needs, which is why it is the default almost everywhere.

Why subnets exist at all

A subnet — short for subnetwork — is simply one of these bounded groups. Dividing a big network into subnets keeps traffic organised: devices can chatter freely within their subnet, but anything destined outside it must go through a router, which acts as the gate between streets. This matters for three practical reasons. It keeps local chatter local, so your laptop's conversation with your printer does not spill across a whole company. It lets administrators apply different rules to different groups — the guest Wi-Fi in its own subnet, unable to see the office machines. And it makes addresses manageable: a block handed to an organisation, as described in how IP addresses are assigned, can be carved into subnets of exactly the sizes needed.

Your home network is one subnet

In a typical home, the router defines a single subnet — usually 192.168.1.0/24 or 192.168.0.0/24 — and hands out device addresses within it via DHCP. This is why the steps in finding your IP on any device always turn up addresses that match except for the last number, and why devices can discover each other (printers, speakers, shared folders) without any configuration: same street, easy visiting. It also explains NAT's place in the picture: NAT translates between this whole private subnet and your single public address, so the outside world sees the street as one house.

Two everyday wrinkles follow from subnetting. First, if you plug in a second router with different settings — say it runs 192.168.2.0/24 — devices on one subnet cannot see devices on the other, which is the classic "the printer vanished" mystery after a network upgrade. Second, guest networks on modern routers are deliberately separate subnets, so visitors get internet access while your own devices stay invisible to them.

Do you ever need to change yours?

Almost never. Home defaults are sensible, and subnet settings only become relevant if you run out of addresses (unlikely outside a smart-home obsession), want to isolate groups of devices deliberately, or connect two networks that happen to use the same subnet — a real issue when a work VPN uses 192.168.1.0/24 and so does your home, causing the clashes described in IP address conflicts explained. If you do change your home subnet, change it on the router and let DHCP re-issue addresses; every device then picks up a consistent new address on its own. Otherwise, the subnet mask can stay what it is for most people: a quiet piece of plumbing you now, at least, know how to read.

IP Address vs MAC Address: What Is the Difference?

One is your device's location on a network. The other is its serial number. Both matter.

Dig into any device's network settings and you will find two addresses side by side: an IP address and a MAC address. They look vaguely similar — strings of numbers and letters — and they are both called "addresses", which is where the confusion starts. In fact they do completely different jobs. One changes every time you join a new network; the other is fixed to your hardware for life. This article explains what each one is, where each is used, and why the difference matters for everything from connecting to hotel Wi-Fi to understanding what privacy a VPN does and does not give you.

The IP address: where you are

An IP address is a logical address: it describes where a device sits on a network so data can be routed to it. It is assigned when the device joins a network — by the router at home, by the coffee shop's network when you visit, by your mobile carrier on the move — and it changes accordingly. You have a public IP address that the internet sees and private ones used inside local networks, and the same device happily holds several at once. If you need the full picture, what is an IP address is our beginner's guide, and you can see your current public one on the homepage.

The MAC address: who you are

A MAC address (Media Access Control address) is a hardware identifier built into every network interface — each Wi-Fi chip, each Ethernet port — when it is manufactured. It is written as six pairs of hexadecimal characters, like 3C:7A:9F:41:2B:08. The first half identifies the manufacturer of the chip; the second half is effectively a serial number, unique to that interface. A laptop with Wi-Fi and an Ethernet port has two MAC addresses, one per interface. Unlike an IP address, a MAC address is meant to stay with the hardware forever, and it is only ever used on the local network: your router sees your phone's MAC address, but websites never do — the MAC is stripped off and replaced at every hop as traffic crosses networks, long before it reaches the internet.

How they work together

On your home network, the two addresses cooperate. Your router keeps a table matching each device's IP address to its MAC address, and uses a protocol called ARP to ask "who has address 192.168.1.42?" when it needs to deliver something — the device with that IP answers with its MAC, and the data is delivered to the right hardware. Your router's client list, showing the devices on your network, is really a list of these pairings. The pairing is also how some router features work: parental controls and "reserve this address for the printer" settings identify devices by MAC address precisely because it does not change, while IP addresses come and go through DHCP as the assignment process describes.

The practical differences, side by side

  • Permanence: a MAC address is fixed to the hardware; an IP address is lent by each network and changes between networks and over time.
  • Scope: a MAC address works only on the local network segment; an IP address is designed to be routed across the whole internet.
  • Who assigns it: the manufacturer sets the MAC; the network (via DHCP) assigns the IP.
  • Who can see it: only devices on your local network — mainly your router — ever see your MAC. Every website sees your IP.
  • Purpose: the MAC delivers data to the right physical device on a local network; the IP gets data to the right network, and the right device within it, from anywhere in the world.

The privacy twist: MAC randomisation

Because a MAC address never changes, it was historically a tracking risk in the physical world: a phone broadcasting its real MAC while scanning for Wi-Fi could be recognised by networks in shops and stations, building a picture of its owner's movements. Modern phones and laptops therefore randomise their MAC address — presenting a made-up one that differs per network and rotates over time — which is why your router may show a device name like "unknown" and why the steps in finding your IP on any device sometimes show a "private Wi-Fi address" note on iPhone and Android. This is a healthy default; turn it off only for your own home network if a router feature genuinely needs the real MAC.

Which one should you care about?

For anything involving the internet — location, privacy, being blocked, using a VPN — it is the IP address that matters, because it is the only one of the two that leaves your home. For anything inside your home — identifying which device is which, reserving addresses, filtering a child's tablet at the router — it is the MAC address doing the work. Remember it as: the IP is the address of the house you are currently in; the MAC is the serial number engraved on the device itself. Houses change. Serial numbers do not.

Why Does Your IP Address Change When You Restart Your Router?

It is the lease system doing its job — here is exactly what happens at switch-on.

Restart your router, check your IP address, and sometimes it is different from before — and sometimes, maddeningly, it is exactly the same. Both outcomes are normal, and both come from the same machinery. Your public address is not owned by your router; it is borrowed from your provider, and a restart hands it back and asks for one again. Whether you get the same address back depends on timing, your provider's settings, and how long the router was off. This article explains the process step by step, and how to make a change more likely when you actually want one.

Your address is a lease, not a possession

Most home connections use dynamic IP addresses. When your router connects, your provider's DHCP system lends it an address from a pool, together with a lease — an expiry time that might be a few hours or a few weeks away. The full chain of how addresses flow from global registries down to your router is covered in how IP addresses are assigned. The lease is the key to everything that follows: while it is valid, the address is reserved for you; once it lapses, the address returns to the pool.

What happens during a restart

When the router goes off, it simply vanishes from the provider's network — it does not politely hand the address back. The provider's systems still show the address as leased to your connection until the lease expires. When the router comes back on, it asks to reconnect, and here the paths divide:

  • Quick restart, lease still valid: the provider recognises your router and renews the same address. From its point of view, you merely blinked. This is why a ten-second reboot so often changes nothing.
  • Long absence, lease expired: your old address went back into the pool — and may already have been given to another customer. Your router gets the next available address, which is a different one.
  • Provider-side events: maintenance, outages or network reconfiguration can force a fresh assignment regardless of timing, which is why your address sometimes changes overnight without you touching anything.

Why some people's addresses almost never change

Lease lengths vary enormously between providers. Some set leases of a few hours; others set them for weeks and design their systems to keep reissuing the same address to the same router — identified by its MAC address — for stability. Cable providers are often notably "sticky" this way. Nothing is broken in either case; a sticky dynamic address is still dynamic, and can change without warning when the provider upgrades equipment or your router is replaced. If you need an address that is guaranteed fixed, that is a static address, usually a paid business extra — and if you need a guaranteed different address on demand, a VPN is the reliable tool, since restarting may not do it.

How to make a change more likely

If your goal is a fresh address — because the current one is blocked somewhere, or being flooded during a game, the scenarios in what someone can do with your IP address — the practical recipe is patience. Switch the router off and leave it off well past the likely lease window: ten minutes is a reasonable minimum, overnight is much better. When it reconnects, it is asking as a stranger, and the pool will usually hand it something different. Then verify on the homepage tool that the address really changed before assuming the problem is solved — and if it did not change after an overnight wait, your provider is one of the sticky ones, and the remaining options are asking the provider or using a VPN. The full set of methods is compared in how to change your IP address.

Meanwhile, inside your home

A router restart also reshuffles things indoors. Your devices' private addresses are leased by the router itself, and after a restart a device may receive a different local address than it had — which is why a network printer or a home server occasionally "moves" and has to be found again. This is harmless and separate from the public change: websites neither know nor care which private address your laptop holds. If a device ends up fighting another over the same local address, that is the related glitch explained in IP address conflicts.

The short version

Your router borrows its address, and a restart returns the loan and asks again. Ask quickly, and you usually get the same address back; stay away until the lease dies, and you get a fresh one from the pool. Either way, nothing is wrong — the system is doing precisely what it was designed to do.

What Is an IP Address Conflict and How Do You Fix It?

Two devices, one address — why it happens indoors, and the quick way out.

"Windows has detected an IP address conflict." It is one of those error messages that sounds catastrophic and is usually fixed in two minutes. An IP address conflict means two devices on your local network are trying to use the same private IP address at the same time — and since an address is supposed to identify one device, the network no longer knows where to deliver data, so one or both devices lose their connection. Conflicts only happen with private addresses inside your own network; your public address is managed by your provider and cannot clash this way. Here is why conflicts happen, and how to clear one.

Why an address must be unique

Within a network, an IP address is an identity. Your router delivers data by matching addresses to devices, the system described in how NAT works and what a subnet is. If two devices claim 192.168.1.50, a reply meant for one may land on the other, or be dropped as undeliverable. Devices detect the clash — typically by asking on the network "is anyone using this address?" before settling on it — and raise the conflict error rather than fight over it.

The usual causes

  • A manually set address collides with DHCP. Someone gave a device a fixed (static) private address — for a printer or a home server — choosing a number that the router's DHCP pool also hands out automatically. Sooner or later the router lends that same number to another device, and the two collide.
  • A sleeping device wakes up to find its address taken. A device that sleeps for a long time can miss its lease renewal; the router reassigns the address to something else, and the sleeper wakes still believing the address is its own.
  • Two DHCP servers on one network. A second router, an old access point or a misconfigured extender starts handing out addresses alongside the main router, and the two systems — neither knowing what the other has lent — inevitably issue duplicates.
  • A device brought from another network with a hard-coded address from elsewhere, which happens to match one already in use at home.

The assignment machinery behind all of this — leases, pools and renewals — is explained in how IP addresses are assigned.

How to fix it, fastest first

Restart the affected device. When it rejoins the network it asks for an address afresh, and the router gives it a free one. This resolves the large majority of conflicts on the spot.

Restart the router. The nuclear option, and a reliable one: every device reconnects and receives a clean, unique address from a fresh start. Annoying for the household for two minutes, decisive for the problem.

Renew the lease manually. On Windows, open Command Prompt and run ipconfig /release then ipconfig /renew. On a Mac, use the "Renew DHCP Lease" button in the network settings for your connection. On a phone, toggling Wi-Fi off and on, or forgetting and rejoining the network, achieves the same thing. The screens involved are the same ones used in finding your IP on any device, where you can also confirm the device now holds a different address.

Fix the root cause if it keeps happening. If the conflict returns, something structural is wrong. Check for a device with a manually set address inside the router's DHCP range — the proper fix is to move manual assignments outside the pool, or better, create a DHCP reservation in the router settings so the router itself always gives that device the same address and never lends it to anyone else. And hunt down any second router or extender acting as a DHCP server; it should be in access-point or bridge mode instead.

What a conflict is not

A conflict does not mean anyone has hacked your network, and it does not involve the internet at all — it is a local traffic jam between your own devices. It also cannot happen to your public address: that is assigned by your provider to your router alone, and if two of its customers somehow received the same one, the provider's own systems would notice long before you did. One related oddity is worth recognising: a device that cannot get any address may assign itself one starting with 169.254. That is not a conflict but a cry for help — the device could not reach the router's DHCP at all — and restarting the device, then the router, is again the place to start.

How Websites Use Your IP Address (And What They Cannot See)

The everyday jobs your IP quietly does for every site you visit — and its hard limits.

Every website you visit receives your IP address with every request — it cannot work any other way, because the site needs to know where to send the page. But receiving your address and using it are two different things, and most sites put it to work in more ways than visitors realise: some helpful, some protective, some commercial. This article lists the real uses, based on how sites actually operate, and then draws the equally important line: the things your IP address, on its own, never tells them. You can see the address in question on our homepage.

Delivering the right version of the site

The most common use is localisation. From your address, a site estimates your country — reliably — and usually your region, using the databases explained in how websites know your location from your IP. It then serves the matching language, currency, prices, tax rules and delivery options, and honours content licences that differ by country. This is why a shop greets you in your language before you have clicked anything, and why travelling — or using a VPN — suddenly presents you with another country's version of a familiar site.

Security and abuse prevention

Your address is a workhorse of site security. Sites and their protection services use it to spot impossible travel (your account logging in from home and from another continent within the hour), to rate-limit requests so scrapers and bots cannot hammer the site from one address, to enforce blocks on addresses with a history of attacks or spam, and to flag logins from networks known for abuse, such as certain data centres. These systems are blunt — a shared or recycled address can inherit someone else's bad reputation, which is one reason an innocent user occasionally meets a block page — but without address-based checks, the modern web's bot problem would be far worse. Our article on what someone can do with your IP address covers the receiving end of this.

Analytics and rough recognition

Sites record addresses in their logs and analytics to count visits, estimate where their audience is, and detect returning households. Here the limits matter: because everyone in a home shares one public address, and because home addresses are dynamic and change over time, an IP is a fuzzy identifier — good for "roughly how many households in this region visited", poor for "this exact person". Serious tracking relies on accounts and cookies instead, which is why our privacy basics article ranks the IP below those identifiers.

Advertising and content decisions

Advertisers use IP-derived location for the obvious purpose — not advertising snow tyres in a desert, or a shop with no delivery to your country — and streaming and sports services use it to enforce regional rights. Some sites also A/B test or personalise by region. What IP-based advertising cannot do is follow you as an individual with any precision: it knows a household's rough area, nothing about who in the household is browsing, which is why the uncannily specific ads people complain about are almost always the work of cookies and accounts, not addresses.

What websites cannot see from your IP

Now the other side of the ledger. From your IP address alone, a website cannot learn:

  • Your name, email, phone number or home address — the mapping from address to subscriber exists only in your provider's records.
  • Which person or which device in your household is browsing — the address is shared, and your devices' MAC addresses and private addresses never leave your home network.
  • Your precise location — the estimate is a city area at best, and the coordinates in databases are usually a provider hub or an area centre, not a building.
  • Your browsing on other sites, your files, or anything else on your devices.

A site knows far more about you from what you do on the site itself — the account you log into, the things you click, the details you type — than it ever learns from your address. The address is the envelope, not the letter.

How long do sites keep it?

Server logs typically retain addresses for days to months, depending on the site's security needs and legal obligations; analytics data may be kept longer, sometimes with the address shortened or anonymised — regulators in some regions require exactly that. If a site's privacy policy mentions IP addresses, it is almost always in this context: logs, security and statistics. And the practical lever remains yours: a VPN swaps the address sites see for the VPN server's, and a fresh address from your provider resets the household identifier entirely, as how to change your IP address describes. Just remember that neither touches the accounts and cookies that do the heavy lifting of recognition — the address was never the main character in that story.

How the tool works

Your browser contacts a well-known IP detection service, which simply reports back the address it sees — the same address every website sees when you connect. We use more than one service so that if one is down, another can answer, and if none of them can be reached, the tool says so honestly rather than guessing. The location, provider and timezone details come from the same services' databases, and they are estimates: usually right about the country, approximate about the city, and never about your exact address.

What this site is not

WhatIsMyIPHub cannot look up other people's IP addresses, cannot trace an address to a person, and cannot tell you anything about a connection you are not currently using. Any website that promises to find a person's name or home address from an IP is promising something that is not possible from public data. If you have a question the site does not answer, you can reach us through the contact page.

The IP lookup tool

The tool runs in your browser. To show your IP address, your browser contacts third-party IP detection services (such as ipapi.co, ipwho.is and ipify). Those services necessarily see your IP address — that is how the lookup works — and their own privacy policies govern what they do with it. WhatIsMyIPHub itself operates no server and no database for this tool: your result is displayed on your device and is not sent to us or stored by us.

What we collect

This is a static website. We do not run user accounts, we do not set our own tracking cookies, and we do not collect names, email addresses or IP addresses from visitors. If you email us, we receive the address and content of your email, and we use them only to reply.

Hosting and third parties

The site is hosted on GitHub Pages, and the fonts and IP lookup services are provided by third parties. Those providers may process technical information such as your IP address and browser details as part of delivering their services, under their own privacy policies. We do not control that processing.

Your choices

If you do not want your browser to contact the lookup services, simply do not open the homepage tool; the articles and other pages work without it. You can also block the services in your browser or firewall, in which case the tool will show an error message instead of a result.
